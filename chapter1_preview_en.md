# Chapter 1: What Is Wisdom?

## 1. What Is Wisdom?

Wisdom is a complex and profound concept. In the book *The Repository of Wisdom*, wisdom is defined as the ability to see through the essence of things, possess a long-term vision, and demonstrate problem-solving abilities in adversity. Those who can persist in their goals and keep moving forward show strong willpower, not easily shaken. This ability to "meet challenges head-on" is one manifestation of wisdom. They rely not on external techniques, but on inner strength — a calm yet resilient mental power.

For example, in ancient Greek mythology, Odysseus endured ten years of wandering. Facing the temptations of the Sirens and the threats of the Cyclops, he never gave up his belief in returning home. His wisdom lay not only in his cleverness at resolving crises, but also in that resilience in the face of adversity.

This mental power brings to mind the words from the *Tao Te Ching*: "Knowing others is intelligence; knowing yourself is true wisdom." Those who can see through others are intelligent because they see the patterns of the external world; but those who truly understand their own hearts touch the depths of wisdom, because they face the root of life. This is perhaps the first step of wisdom — shifting from external observation to internal awareness.


### The Face of Wisdom

Wisdom has many faces. Every culture and every person depicts it in their own way. Plato once said that wisdom is the highest virtue of the soul, the rational control of desire and passion, the pursuit of truth. The Stoics believed that wisdom is a way of life in harmony with nature and the pursuit of virtue — not being led by emotions, but illuminating the heart with reason. Buddhist "Prajna" wisdom points to the realization of impermanence, the emptiness of all things, and the cycle of cause and effect, a lamp on the path to liberation. And that verse in the Book of Proverbs, "The fear of the Lord is the beginning of wisdom," reminds us that wisdom may begin with a reverence for life and a higher unknown.

Psychologists have also tried to outline wisdom. Paul Baltes said that wisdom is a rich insight into life, the ability to deal with uncertainty, the judgment to find balance in complex situations, including reflecting on oneself, transcending oneself, and even deep empathy for others. Robert Sternberg pointed out that wisdom is the art of seeking balance among the individual, others, and the environment — it transcends mere self-concern, exploring the path of harmonious coexistence in a broader dimension.

These different faces are like pieces of a puzzle, assembling the multifaceted nature of wisdom.


### Self-Knowledge and Transcendence

Wisdom always seems inseparable from the matter of "knowing oneself." To truly enter wisdom, perhaps we need to first stop and see what is hidden in our hearts.

In Maslow's theory of "self-transcendence," wise people possess a special ability: they can accept different opinions without rushing to defend themselves; they remain calm in the face of challenges and are good at finding meaning in various emotions. This acceptance and reflection come from their deep dialogue with their inner selves, finding a foothold within. As another ancient saying goes: "He who conquers others is strong; he who conquers himself is mighty." Conquering others relies on external strength, but true strength is conquering oneself, being able to face one's fears, and loosening the shackles of past imprints in the subconscious. This self-conquest is not accomplished overnight; it relies on the gradual accumulation of every small moment of self-awareness.

This awareness of the self also lends great weight to Laozi's words. The *Tao Te Ching* says: "The reason the sage surpasses ordinary people is not because he knows everything, but because he is aware of his own ignorance." Those who think they are omniscient may instead fall into blindness, like a disease that cannot be detected, easily consumed by arrogance.

And those who admit their limitations are instead willing to maintain a mindset of continuous exploration — is this not the starting point of wisdom? Recognizing one's boundaries is the only way to move toward broader horizons.

As Siddhartha elaborated after his enlightenment into the state of "oneness," wisdom is a capability, a profound art — it endows us with the ability to think harmoniously in every moment, to deeply feel the unity of life, and to quietly merge into it. Siddhartha reached this state precisely because he deeply knew himself, and then successfully transcended himself. Wisdom grows subtly in this mutual blending of self-knowledge and transcendence.


### The Goal of Wisdom

What is the goal of wisdom? Perhaps it is not some unfathomable answer, but guiding us to live more authentically and more freely. *Conversations with God* once pointed out that wisdom means becoming an awakened one, a state in which one can respond to the question "Who am I?" at any moment. After Siddhartha attained wisdom, he said: "My only task is to love this world." He no longer despised the world, nor hated himself, but regarded everything with love and reverence — including himself. I very much like this view. Wisdom allows us to see the heavens and the earth, see all living beings, and also see ourselves. It lets us appreciate the flight of a bird, gaze at a newborn baby, and feel the miracle of life.

In my view, the core of wisdom is to know oneself, to see through the essence of the luminous nature of one's true self, to transcend oneself, and to live the life one wants. It is not cold rules, but a source of living water that brings happiness and benefit to people. You can choose any wisdom that suits you — as long as it is reasonable and brings joy, it is worth embracing. Narrow vision only blocks a broader sky. If we stubbornly believe that only our own beliefs are the truth, then conflict and strife are inevitable.

Wisdom need not be forced. Those who do not like it need not be compelled to use it; those who oppose it need not waste effort refuting it. Just let it enter our world and bring happiness to those who can discern its meaning.


## 2. The Journey of Self-Transcendence: From Camel to Child

In Nietzsche's classic work *Thus Spoke Zarathustra*, he proposed the famous theory of the "Three Metamorphoses of the Spirit," using the camel, the lion, and the child as three images to symbolize the three stages of human spiritual development. This is not only a philosophical metaphor, but also a path to self-transcendence. So, what do these three stages mean? How do they guide us from knowing ourselves to transcending ourselves?


### First Metamorphosis: The Camel — The Burden of "Thou Shalt"

When you think of a camel, what comes to mind? It is that figure in the endless yellow sand, silently carrying a heavy load, head bowed, moving forward. The characteristic of the camel is submission; it willingly accepts external whipping and commands, bearing loads exceeding its own weight. In the first stage of the Three Metamorphoses, the human spirit is like the camel, in a passive state of "thou shalt." External voices — parental expectations, social norms, other people's values — constantly tell you "you should do this and that," and you can only passively accept and silently follow.

At this stage, we are like camels carrying heavy loads, our vision blocked by the sand flying under our feet, unable to see the distance, with no time to listen to the voice within.

Remember when your parents might have said to you as a child: "You must be obedient, study hard, and find a stable job in the future." At that time, you had not yet formed independent judgment and could only follow orders. The spirit of the camel is precisely the symbol of this passive submission.

We think that the more we bear, the more we prove our worth. We take other people's truths as our own beliefs, seeking security in submission. It embodies a typical state in the early stage of human life: passive acceptance, blind obedience, the spirit not yet awakened.


### Second Metamorphosis: The Lion — The Awakening of "I Will"

However, the camel will not forever be satisfied with trekking through the desert. As the spirit develops, it will eventually tear off the heavy load on its back, grow claws, and become a lion. What is the lion? It is a symbol of strength, majesty, and freedom, the "king of beasts." Unlike the camel's submission, the lion pursues a state of "I will" — an active will to seize the dominance of life, a rebellion against traditional values.

But to transform from a camel into a lion is no easy task. Nietzsche designed a battle full of symbolism for this transformation: the camel must face the dragon in the desert, and the dragon's name is "Thou Shalt." Interestingly, "Thou Shalt" is the ultimate embodiment of the camel spirit. On the surface, this battle is the camel's confrontation with the external world, but in essence, it is the camel's struggle with its inner self. Only by defeating "Thou Shalt," that is, overcoming the deeply ingrained inertia of submission within, can the camel complete its transformation and become a lion.

The spirit of the lion symbolizes the awakening of life. We learn to say "no" — no to false morality, no to imposed meaning, no to a life that does not belong to us. It no longer blindly follows, no longer remains silent, but declares the will to dominate one's own life with the roar of "I will": I want freedom, I want individuality, I want to break free from old shackles.

The lion's awakening overthrows traditional constraints. This is a crucial transitional stage on the road to self-transcendence.


### Third Metamorphosis: The Child — The Fulfillment of "I Am"

The highest realm of the spirit is to transform from the lion into the child. Nietzsche believed that the child symbolizes innocence, openness, and infinite possibility. The child's mind is like a blank sheet of paper, free from the constraints of old values and past shackles, welcoming life with the posture of a creator. He regarded the child as a perfect beginning — returning to the origin of life, from which to build a brand-new self and world.

The child's state represents the transcendent realm of "I am," facing the world purely with the heart of a newborn. The simplicity of the child is so touching precisely because it has no distracting thoughts, but is a state of complete acceptance and pure joy in the present moment. In this state, life radiates true inner power.

However, reaching a state similar to that of the child is by no means easy. Nietzsche pointed out that this requires profound life experience and practice — returning to simplicity after experiencing the vicissitudes of life. Only by re-examining the world and oneself without preconceived notions can one, like a child, purely love life, bravely explore the unknown, and calmly enjoy every process, regardless of the outcome.

Nietzsche's Three Metamorphoses is not only a philosophical insight but also echoes Eastern wisdom. The *Tao Te Ching* says: "He who is filled with virtue is like a newborn infant. Poisonous insects will not sting him; ferocious beasts will not seize him; birds of prey will not strike him." A person of true virtue is like a newborn infant, innocent and pure, always winning the trust of nature and people's hearts. Whether it is Nietzsche's "child" or Laozi's "newborn infant," both point to a clear and powerful state of life — after the trials of the world, returning to the original intention, moving toward true maturity. This state is the unity of strength and gentleness, and also a true radiance of character.


### The Writing Approach of This Book

Nietzsche's Three Metamorphoses reveals the journey of self-transcendence, and this book, *The Complete Wisdom — From Knowing Yourself to Transcending Yourself*, takes this as its framework, leading readers from the "camel" toward the "child." The outline of this book is as follows:

- Healing the Camel: In the beginning, we are like camels, bearing heavy responsibilities and the expectations of "thou shalt," our hearts often wounded. Healing ourselves and releasing the burden of suppression is the starting point for moving toward self-transformation.
- Awakening the Lion: Awakening from blind submission, recognizing the true self — that infinitely complete nature, rediscovering inner strength and the control over one's own destiny.
- Returning to the Child: Embracing innocence, living in the present with the posture of "I am," freely creating one's own world, returning to the love of life and inner joy.

# Chapter 2: Embarking on the Journey of Self-Healing (Excerpt)

Have you ever felt that life seems to have fallen into some kind of cycle that is difficult to break free from? The same predicaments play out repeatedly, the same pain lingers, and even certain patterns of the family inadvertently influence your choices. This involuntary repetition makes one cannot help but ask: Are we destined to repeat this established "fate"? The psychologist Jung once profoundly pointed out: "Until you make the unconscious conscious, it will direct your life and you will call it fate." This statement warns us that behind seemingly sober and rational choices, the power of the subconscious is secretly dominant. Those emotions, beliefs, and fears hidden deep within are like invisible undercurrents, silently determining the trajectory of our lives. Only when we muster the courage to deeply explore this unknown realm can we find the key to change.


### A True Story

Xiao Wei, 27 years old, had just experienced her fifth breakup. Every time, the reason her boyfriends gave for breaking up was strikingly similar: "Being with you is too exhausting, I feel like I can't breathe." Even the most recent relationship, which she had been full of expectations for and thought could finally "reach a happy ending," ended in the same way. The successive blows plunged Xiao Wei into deep self-doubt. She was confused, powerless, feeling trapped in a pattern of repeated failure, unable to break free.

Under the guidance of her counselor, she slowly opened the floodgates of memory, and the scenes of her childhood rushed in like a tide. At that time, her father was often away on business, and her mother shouldered the entire burden of the family alone, physically and mentally exhausted. Xiao Wei, at that young age, did not yet know how to interpret the exhaustion and anxiety in her mother's eyes; she only knew that she was always living cautiously — because the slightest mistake would bring a barrage of blame, followed by days of coldness and neglect. These experiences buried the seed of "I am not worthy of being loved, and will eventually be abandoned" deep in her young heart. After reaching adulthood, this seed of fear took root and sprouted in Xiao Wei's intimate relationships. The subconscious fear of being abandoned made her extremely sensitive and anxious in relationships, like a startled bird. To avoid being abandoned, in every relationship she clung tightly to the other person, message bombing becoming the norm — "Where are you? Why haven't you come back yet? Who did you meet today?" This suffocating sense of control and oppression反而 accelerated the boyfriends' departure.

Every breakup was like a cruel prophecy, validating the "curse" deep within her heart: "See, I've been abandoned after all." She helplessly fell into the quagmire of "repetition compulsion," unable to break free.

Later, Xiao Wei began to engage with psychology. Through profound self-awareness, she started trying to change the deep-rooted old patterns in her subconscious, breaking those constantly repeating cycles of pain. As the inner fear gradually dissipated, she finally gained the ability to build and maintain healthy relationships, and her path of love began to smooth out.


### Why Is It So Hard to "Know but Not Do"?

We often say, "I understand all the principles, yet still can't live this life well." Behind this lies the enormous power of the subconscious. Human consciousness consists of two parts: the conscious and the subconscious. The conscious is responsible for rational thinking and judgment, which we can clearly perceive. The subconscious, on the other hand, is like an invisible background program, quietly running, governing our intuition, emotions, habits, and those beliefs and fears hidden beneath consciousness. In psychological healing, the subconscious plays a crucial role. Relying solely on rational analysis and preaching often cannot touch the root of the problem, because it cannot bypass those deep-seated complexes. As Jung said: "Knowledge has its value within its own domain, yet once it crosses the boundary, it turns into illusion and deception." Just like Xiao Wei in the case, she clearly knew that "holding on too tightly is not good," yet she could not control her own behavior, because what truly controlled her actions was those deep-seated fears that had not yet been perceived. To change this situation, one must go deep into the subconscious and reshape the inner patterns. Currently, there are mainly two commonly used methods in psychology: one is through hypnosis or meditation, allowing the brain to enter a calm state to directly dialogue with the subconscious, deeply healing those suppressed emotions and limiting beliefs; the other is cognitive behavioral therapy, gradually reshaping the content of the subconscious through repeated practice of positive thinking and behavior. The former is like diving into the deep sea, confronting the inner undercurrents; the latter is like the silent nourishment of spring rain. The two complement each other, together helping us rewrite the "code" deep within and open up a brand-new trajectory for life.
